The aberration function

A perfect lens turns the incoming plane wave into a spherical wave converging on one point. A real lens adds a phase error that depends on the angle ω at which an electron leaves the optic axis and on its azimuth φ. This error is the aberration function χ(ω, φ). This widget uses the convention

χ(ω,φ) = (2π/λ) [ ½C₁ω² + ½A₁ω² cos 2(φ−φA1) + ⅓C₂₁ω³ cos(φ−φ21) + ⅓A₂ω³ cos 3(φ−φA2) + ¼C₃ω⁴ + ⅙C₅ω⁶ ]

Each coefficient is a length. An aberration of order n multiplies ωn+1, so higher-order terms are negligible at small angles and take over as the aperture α opens. The probe in the specimen plane is the inverse Fourier transform of the aperture function A(ω) with that phase applied:

ψ(r) = F⁻¹[ A(ω) · e−iχ(ω) ],    probe intensity = |ψ(r)|²

This is the same Fourier relationship between the back focal plane and the image plane that you meet in the diffraction-physics chapter of the TEM course, now with a phase plate in the back focal plane.

Prefactor conventions differ between textbooks and between corrector software. The usual trap is coma: with the ⅓ prefactor used here, CEOS-style B₂ is C₂₁/3. Always check which convention a paper uses before comparing numbers.

Defocus C₁

A parabolic phase, the same in every direction. On its own it just moves the focus. Its real use is balancing: a negative C₁ (underfocus) cancels part of the ω⁴ rise from positive C₃ across the aperture. The button "Minimise rms phase with C₁" picks the C₁ giving the smallest rms phase error across the aperture: C₁ = −½C₃α² − 0.3C₅α⁴. For an uncorrected probe at its optimum aperture α ≈ (4λ/C₃)¼, this reduces to the familiar C₁ ≈ −√(C₃λ).

Two-fold astigmatism A₁

The focal length differs between two perpendicular directions, so a round probe becomes elongated, and on either side of focus it turns into a line along one direction or the other. It is the aberration you correct most often at the microscope, using the stigmators. Because it grows only as ω², it matters even at small apertures. Its tolerance tightens as 1/α²: a few nm is harmless at 10 mrad and ruinous at 30 mrad.

Axial coma C₂₁

An odd, one-fold term: one side of the aperture is advanced, the other retarded. The probe acquires a comet-like tail in the direction φ21. In the aberration function this is axial coma. It is not the off-axis coma of light optics. It appears when the beam is tilted relative to the coma-free axis of the objective lens, which is why coma-free alignment is part of every high-resolution session. For a weak-phase object, odd terms do not change the size of the transfer in the CTF. They shift the phases of the transferred Fourier components, which smears and displaces the image detail.

Three-fold astigmatism A₂

A three-lobed, ω³ phase error that gives triangular probe distortion. It comes from small imperfections and misalignments in the lenses and from parasitic fields inside the corrector itself. In uncorrected instruments it is usually hidden behind C₃. Once C₃ is corrected and the aperture is opened, A₂ is often among the first residuals that must be tuned.

Spherical aberration C₃ and Scherzer's theorem

Rays far from the axis are focused more strongly than paraxial rays, giving a round ω⁴ phase error and a probe with a bright core and a wide halo. Scherzer showed in 1936 that any round, static, space-charge-free electron lens has C₃ > 0, so it cannot be removed by better lens design. It limited TEM resolution for 60 years. Correction needs non-round optics: either a pair of hexapoles (Rose, Haider) or a quadrupole–octupole system (Krivanek). These introduce a negative C₃ while cancelling their own lower-order side effects. With C₃ gone, the aperture can open from ~10 to ~30 mrad. C₅ and the residual A₁, C₂₁ and A₂ then set the new limit.

Chromatic aberration Cc

The lens focal length depends on electron energy. A beam with energy spread ΔE therefore sees a spread of defoci with standard deviation σf = Cc·(ΔE/E)·(1+E/E₀)/(1+E/2E₀), where ΔE is converted from FWHM to an rms value and E₀ = 511 keV. These are incoherent: the probe is the intensity-weighted sum of probes at each defocus. That sum is computed here with Gauss–Hermite quadrature. In HRTEM it gives the temporal-coherence envelope Et(q) = exp[−½(πλσfq²)²], which sets the information limit. Cc matters most at low voltage, where ΔE/E is largest. That is why a Cs corrector alone is not enough at 60 kV, and monochromators and Cc correctors exist.

The π/4 criterion

A wavefront behaves as essentially perfect while its phase error stays within a band of ±π/4 about its mean (the Rayleigh quarter-wave criterion). A constant phase offset changes nothing, so it is the spread of χ that counts, not its value. The dashed ring on the phase map is the largest aperture satisfying this for the current aberrations. Opening the aperture past it adds current but no resolution: the Strehl ratio drops and d₅₀ stops shrinking. The stat tile reports it directly.

HRTEM: phase-contrast transfer

For a thin weak-phase object, each spatial frequency q = ω/λ is transferred with amplitude sin χs(q), where χs is the even (centrosymmetric) part of χ. The odd terms C₂₁ and A₂ only phase-shift the transfer. With the sign convention here, Scherzer defocus (C₁ ≈ −1.2√(C₃λ)) gives a broad band where sin χ ≈ −1, so atoms appear dark. The first zero crossing defines the point resolution, ≈ 0.66 C₃¼λ¾. Textbooks differ in the overall sign of χ, so check before comparing figures.

Try this

  1. Balance. Load Uncorrected · Gaussian focus and press Minimise rms phase with C₁. Adding a second aberration made the probe better. Use the phase map to explain why.
  2. Optimum aperture. In Uncorrected · balanced, sweep α from 4 to 20 mrad, pressing the balance button at each step. Where does d₅₀ bottom out? Compare with the π/4-limited aperture and with (4λ/C₃)¼.
  3. Dumbbells. Switch the right panel to the Si ⟨110⟩ image (dumbbell spacing 1.36 Å). Can any α resolve the dumbbells at 300 kV without correction? Now load Cs-corrected.
  4. Tolerances. In Cs-corrected, raise A₁ until the Strehl ratio falls to 0.8. Repeat at α = 15 mrad. How does the tolerable A₁ scale with α? Predict the scaling for C₂₁ and A₂ before you check.
  5. Equal phase, unequal damage. In the comparison cards, which aberration does the most damage for the same edge phase? Look at how the phase is distributed across the aperture, not just its maximum.
  6. Chromatic limit. Load 60 kV · chromatic limit. Lower ΔE from 0.6 eV (Schottky source) to 0.3 eV (cold FEG) and to 0.1 eV (monochromated). Then go back to 0.6 eV and switch to 300 kV. Which helps more, and why?
  7. Balancing C₅. In Cs-corrected, open α to 35 mrad. Find a small negative C₃, together with C₁, that recovers the probe. This is Scherzer balancing one order higher.

What this model leaves out

  • The source is a point. Finite source size (partial spatial coherence) would broaden every probe further.
  • Only the aberrations listed are included. Real correctors also leave star aberration S₃, four-fold A₃, and higher orders.
  • The ADF image is a thin incoherent object convolved with the probe intensity. There is no channelling or multiple scattering (see the multislice lecture in the TEM course).
  • The CTF ignores the spatial-coherence envelope and specimen drift.
